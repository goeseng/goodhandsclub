Good Hands Club

The best chapter isn’t behind you.

A Korean adaptation of the Men’s Sheds model — a task-based community for retired men in their sixties.

A community for men in their sixties who’d rather build what’s next than look back. We learn new skills with our hands, form easy friendships shoulder to shoulder, and put those hands to good use — for ourselves, for the people close to us, and for the community around us. No lectures, no pity — just good work and good company.

01 · Why now?

Open space can go two ways.

In Korea, a man’s sixties often arrive with a strange kind of freedom: the calendar clears, the title on the business card retires, and the daily crew disperses. That’s a lot of open space — and open space can go two ways.

Left to chance, it drifts toward fewer roles, thinner networks, and days without a reason to leave the house. Korea’s second-wave baby boomers (born 1968–1974) — some 8 million people — are reaching that threshold now, and for men the drop-off in connection after retirement is especially steep.

But open space is also room to build. The same moment can become the start of the most interesting chapter yet — if there’s somewhere worth going, and good people to go there with.

02 · Key Insights

Design the role and the relationships together, and the rest follows.

Five findings from alumni mentoring and field interviews that shape the Good Hands Club model.

Finding 1

Lead with usefulness, not need — and people opt in.

48% of pre-survey respondents named “whether the activity will match my level” as their biggest barrier to entry. The real hesitation isn’t stigma — it’s “will I be any good at this?” Frame the invitation around skill and contribution, and that hesitation turns into curiosity.

Finding 2

Somewhere worth going, on a rhythm.

Retirement quietly takes away a place of one’s own. A workshop you can drop into on a regular schedule — same day, same crew — is what turns a one-off visit into the kind of steady habit that actually shifts outcomes.

Finding 3

The people around a man carry the nudge that makes it stick.

64% consult their spouse first when deciding on a new activity. So we design for that: welfare centres and volunteer hubs act as anchor spaces, and partner- and peer-driven referral becomes part of the model rather than an afterthought.

Finding 4

The two-year mark is the window.

“The first year feels fine. It’s around the one-year point that the boredom creeps in,” one interviewee told us. We aim to reach men while that early freedom is still fresh — and turn it into something to build on, before it goes flat.

Finding 5

Staged, but never a hierarchy.

Old workplace pecking orders re-emerge fast in male-only spaces. A flat, three-step arc — skills, roles, contribution — keeps things horizontal and keeps engagement high: everyone’s learning, everyone’s useful, nobody’s the boss.

03 · The Model

How it works — skills, mates, purpose.

We start with the hands. Learning a real, practical skill — cooking, mending, making — rebuilds the quiet confidence that comes from being good at something. That confidence is easier to find shoulder to shoulder than face to face, so we build it together, as a crew. And once you’ve got skill and company, the natural next step is to put them to use.

Skill gives you the confidence. The crew gives you the belonging. Purpose gives you a reason to keep showing up.
STEP 1
Skills

Learn something with your hands

Cooking, mending, making — real, practical skills

The invitation is a “useful task,” not a support group. Everyday skills that let a man walk in without pretence. Completing one small task restores the quiet certainty of “I can still do this.”

STEP 2
Mates

Shoulder to shoulder, not face to face

Easy friendships built through shared work

Confidence is easier to find alongside people than across from them. Same day, same crew, same workshop — we build it together. Old workplace hierarchies stay out; everyone’s learning, everyone’s useful.

STEP 3
Purpose

Put those hands to good use

For yourself, your people, your neighbourhood

Cooking for the people you love, sharing what you’ve made with the neighbourhood, showing up where you’re needed. Role stipends, activity fees and a “usefulness currency” give contribution real weight — and staying power.

“We’re not here to pass the time. We’re here to learn something with our hands, stand alongside people who get it, and stay useful — on our own terms. Turns out that’s a pretty good way to spend your sixties.”
04 · Differentiation

A different starting point than existing programmes.

A space for everyone A space that fits men in their sixties Open-to-all workshops sound inclusive, but “everyone’s welcome” often means “no one in particular.” Good Hands Club is built so that men in their sixties feel capable and at home from the very first visit.
Simple participation The recovery of a role Most programmes hand out predefined tasks and hope people slot in. We do the reverse: we start from the skills and experience each man already carries, and build real roles around them. Not a seat to fill — a role to hold.
Unpaid volunteering A structure of recognition Goodwill alone burns out. Role stipends, activity fees, and a community currency give contribution real weight — and real staying power. Being useful should count for something, and here it literally does.
05 · Validation

Measured, honestly, in three stages.

From entry through sustained impact, each phase is bound by specific indicators — so we know what is actually working.

Stage 1 · Entry

Short workshop

  • Enrolment conversion rate 25–30%+
  • Distribution of intake channels tracked
  • One-person, one-outcome completion 90%+
  • Intent to enrol in 8-week programme 60%+
Stage 2 · Core hypothesis

8-week main programme

  • Per-session attendance 70% / completion 70%+
  • Significant reduction on loneliness scale (K-DJGLS, p<.05)
  • Priority: change on social-loneliness subscale
  • Re-participation or peer referral intent 30%+
Stage 3 · Sustaining

Long-term impact

  • Self-organised operational roles 30%+
  • Identity shift: "I am someone who ___"
  • Community deliveries, exhibitions, or sales
  • 6-month re-measurement to confirm retention
About Us

The Asan Frontier Academy — and us.

The Asan Frontier Academy is a seven-month leadership programme run by the Asan Nanum Foundation, one of Korea’s leading social-innovation foundations, developing the next generation of social-sector leaders.

Good Hands Club is the real-world project of Team 3 — a group of us brought together through the Academy, with backgrounds across community welfare, cooperatives, public volunteering, and social ventures. We’re prototyping this together during the programme, and building it to last long past the finish line.

06 · Coalition

Five organisations, one shared workshop.

Good Hands Club is a coalition of community welfare centres, a social cooperative, a public volunteer centre, a Catholic nonprofit, and social ventures — committed to running and growing this well beyond a pilot.

It’s a deliberate mix, not a loose alliance: the reach to find the men, the anchor spaces to gather them, the operating models to stay self-reliant, and the hands-on know-how to actually run the workshop. Everything a shed needs to open — and to keep its doors open.
